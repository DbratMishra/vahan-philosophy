The idea that holds it all together

Most performance systems are built to sort people. Ours is built to move everyone.

Most performance systems are built to sort people: to decide who is ahead and who is behind. Ours is built to do something harder and more worthwhile. We want to move the entire curve to the right, so that the whole company gets stronger over time, and every single person has a clear, visible path to move their own line forward. We are not here to rank you. We are here to grow you, and to grow the company through you.

Exhibit · The curve, and where the energy goes

A weak system

A strong system

That single image is the entire ambition of this philosophy.

A great performance system makes the right thing to do the obvious thing to do.

The foundational idea

Two simple truths make it work.

Both are well established in how the best operating companies run. The first is focus: a small number of things, pursued relentlessly, beats a long list pursued half-heartedly. The second is speed of feedback: people improve fastest when they know quickly how they are doing - not once a year, but as they go. Everything in this document is an expression of those two truths. Keep them in mind and the rest follows naturally.

Part One

Goal Setting

What we point our energy at, and how each person sees their part in it.

The five mother metrics

Everything ladders up to a very small set of numbers.

We call them the five mother metrics. They are the numbers we repeat in every business review, every team huddle, and every one-to-one - until they become the language the company uses to talk to itself. The discipline is not in choosing them once; it is in protecting them from the endless temptation to add a sixth and a seventh. Five numbers, held with conviction, will always out-perform fifteen held loosely.

How the best operate

This is not a Vahan invention - it is the dominant pattern in high-growth companies. Meta organises around active users, Airbnb around nights booked, Uber around weekly rides, Shopify around active merchants and GMV. The widely cited rule is one North Star plus roughly three to five supporting measures; more dilutes attention and produces analysis paralysis. We are aligning with what the most disciplined operators already treat as ordinary.

MetaAirbnbUberShopify

Each mother metric belongs to the whole company, and then cascades. A company number becomes a vertical's number, then a team's, then a person's - growing narrower in scope but never changing in kind. This is what gives everyone an unbroken line of sight: you can always trace your own work upward to one of the five. And we hold to one firm rule that keeps that line honest.

No one is ever more than three steps from a company metric.

If your goal sits four steps down, the connection to what the company actually cares about has become too thin to feel. Three degrees is the limit. It guarantees that no one's work disappears into a gap, and that every person can answer a simple question: how does what I did today move one of the five?

The three kinds of goals

Not every goal is the same shape.

Pretending otherwise is where most goal-setting goes wrong. At Vahan a goal is always one of three types, and naming the type up front changes how we treat it - because a contribution is judged on how far the number moved, a milestone on whether it shipped, and an enabler on whether the capability now exists. Holding someone to a hard number on something that was always a build is one of the quiet ways performance systems lose people's trust.

Enabler

A capability or input that drives a metric without summing into it - the groundwork that makes future numbers move.

Judged onWhether the capability now exists.
Naming the type keeps the conversation honest, and the judgement fair.

How a goal is written

Every goal carries five simple tags.

So that anyone reading it understands not just the target but the thinking behind it. None of this is bureaucracy - each tag answers a question a thoughtful person would ask anyway, and writing it down once saves a hundred conversations later. And each person holds only three to four primary goals. More than that is not ambition; it is dilution.

Part Two

Performance Cadence

The rhythm that keeps goals alive, and the data that makes them real.

A goal set in January and reviewed in December is not a goal; it is a wish with a deadline.

Performance is something that happens in the weeks between, which is why the rhythm matters as much as the targets. Vahan runs on two beats.

The weekly beatWeekly · light

Catch drift while it's cheap

Short, forward-looking, and light. It is not a status report or an interrogation. Kept light, it builds a continuous record almost by itself - and asks three questions, no more:

What moved?
What is stuck?
What changes this week?
The periodic beatPeriodic · structured

Step back and calibrate

Less frequent and more structured. We look at the trend across the mother metrics, calibrate fairly across teams, and have the fuller conversation about growth and direction.

Because the weekly beat has been quietly building a record all along, the periodic review is a summary of what already happened, not a scramble to reconstruct the year from memory.

The data architecture beneath it

We don't wait for perfect plumbing to start seeing clearly.

None of this rhythm works if the numbers are not trustworthy and close at hand, so each metric is wired to a source. The honest position is that we start with a mix, and that is by design. The dashboard is live from day one even if some feeds are manual - and as our systems mature, we upgrade individual feeds underneath without ever disturbing the goals on top.

How the best operate

The single annual review has been steadily abandoned. Continuous performance conversations are now standard practice at scale - adopted by Microsoft, Adobe and Deloitte, among others. The evidence is unusually consistent: organisations with continuous feedback report materially lower turnover, and employees who receive feedback weekly are several times more likely to be engaged. Frequent conversation allows course correction while it is still cheap, and reduces the recency bias that distorts a once-a-year assessment.

Part Three

Decision Making

How we recognise the exceptional, support the struggling, and act with honesty and speed.

A performance philosophy is only as real as the decisions it drives. It is easy to measure and never act; the hard, important part is being willing to call performance clearly, in both directions, and to do it with care. Vahan makes two kinds of high-performance callouts, and treats them as equally important.

↗ Calling out the exceptional

Name it, specifically and visibly

When someone is genuinely moving their line - and through it the company's - we say so. Recognition that is vague or rationed loses its power. We name what the person did, tie it to the metric it moved, and make it something others can see and aspire to. Lifting the curve depends on the right tail having room and reason to keep going.

↘ Calling out what is not working

Early, kindly, with a path

The harder callout, and the one that protects everyone, is naming when performance is consistently below the line. We do this early, kindly, and with a genuine path to improve - because the alternative, silence followed by a sudden exit, is the cruelest thing a company can do to a person. It gives someone the chance to move their line while there is still time.

The kindest thing and the fairest thing are usually the same thing: tell people the truth early.

The decision frameworks

Three simple frameworks keep the calls fair and free of bias.

The common thread is that decisions rest on the accumulated record against the mother metrics - not on the most recent week or the loudest voice. Evidence over impression, every time. That is what lets people trust that a callout, in either direction, was earned.

i

Evidence over impression

Decisions rest on the documented trail across the period, not on the most recent week or the most visible moment.

ii

Pattern over event

One bad week is noise; a sustained trend is signal. We act on direction and consistency, not on single data points.

iii

Context before conclusion

Before any callout, we ask what the goal type was and what conditions surrounded it - so we judge like with like.

Part Four

Evaluation & Rewards

How performance connects to growth, recognition, and reward - fairly and transparently.

Evaluation is the moment all of the above comes together. Done well, it should contain no surprises, because the weekly and periodic beats have made the picture clear all year. The evaluation is simply the formal reading of a story everyone already knew was being written.

What we evaluate on

01

Movement against the metrics

Did your line move, by how much, and in the direction that matters most.

02

How the results were achieved

Performance and the way we work are judged together - never one at the expense of the other.

03

Growth over the period

Progress and trajectory count, not just the final position on the curve.

We reward movement, not just position.

That third point is the heart of a curve-moving philosophy. A person who started on the left and travelled a long way to the right has done something we value enormously - even if they have not yet passed someone who began ahead of them. This is how a company lifts its whole distribution rather than simply rewarding those who arrived already strong.

Reward the distance travelled, not only the finishing line. Multiplied across everyone, that movement is the whole point.

How rewards connect

Rewards follow the evaluation transparently, in line with our compensation philosophy, so that the link between performance and reward is something people can see and trust rather than guess at. The exceptional are recognised meaningfully. Steady, reliable contribution is valued honestly. And real growth - the movement of a line to the right - is seen and rewarded, because that movement, multiplied across everyone, is the whole point.

Reward the movement, not only the position. That is how a whole company rises.

In one breath

The whole philosophy, said quickly.

We focus on a few numbers that truly matter. We make sure everyone can see how their work connects to them. We talk about performance often, honestly, and early, so feedback arrives while it can still help. We make decisions on evidence, in both directions, with care. And we reward movement - the lifting of every line to the right - because a company that moves its whole curve forward is a company that wins, and takes its people with it.

Everything else - the templates, the dashboards, the cadences - is just machinery in service of one idea: move the curve, not just the people.
